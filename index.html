Train the tutor 🎓

Chatbots like ChatGPT learned to be “helpful” from people who compared two answers and picked the better one. Today, that person is you. You will teach a language-learning chatbot what a good answer looks like, and then watch what it does with what you taught it.

🖱️

See training in action

Experience how human preferences, one click at a time, shape what a chatbot says: the “reinforcement learning” Floridi mentions.

🤖

Agency without intelligence

Watch a system make choices “successfully” without understanding them, or checking whether they are true.

🌎

Whose values?

Reflect on whose ideas about “good English” end up inside the AI tools our learners use.

3 · What just happened

You were the labor

Comparing answers like this is how ChatGPT’s predecessor, InstructGPT, was trained. OpenAI hired about 40 contractors to do it (Ouyang et al., 2022). Floridi credits this “reinforcement learning” for ChatGPT’s leap (p. 5), but not the people.

Agency without intelligence

Your tutor chose answers, and it will keep choosing them for every learner who asks. It never checked whether anything was true: it only knows what you rewarded. That is what Floridi means by acting successfully without understanding (pp. 5–6).

Whose English?

Bender and colleagues ask us to identify “whose values are expressed and supported through a technology” (p. 619). Now you know. A tutor used by millions of learners would have the values of a few dozen raters.

Try training a strict one, or a very chatty one, and see how its answers change.

Compare tutors

Share your tutor’s profile in the forum. How is it different from your classmates’? Whose learners would it serve best?

Were you consistent?

Did you reward the same things every time? A real rater makes thousands of these choices a day. What would that do to their judgement?

Who should rate?

If you could choose the people who train a tutor for your learners, who would they be: teachers, learners, linguists, speakers of which varieties?

What this is, and what it isn’t. A real reward model is itself a large neural network that learns from the text of the answers. Ours is a toy: each answer has been described by hand on five features, and the tutor learns how much you value each one (a simple version of the same statistics, called a Bradley–Terry model). The principle is the real one: people compare answers, and the model learns to produce what they prefer, whether or not it is true.
